This section guides you through the principles of  Roche photography and can serve as a basis for a photographer briefing.
Authenticity and staging
Authenticity
All Roche images should convey this key trait. By feeling authentic, the images can tell a meaningful and true story about the people and processes within Roche.

People looking towards the camera
When people look towards the camera, the image captured gives an emotional connection that feels honest.

People looking away from the camera
When people involved in tasks are captured, ensure their gestures appear natural, not forced or staged. Capturing a subject unnoticed (while they are talking to someone but not out of the frame) can aid authenticity.

People in conversation
In order to photograph people in discussion or any type of collaboration, we desire them to look engaged and authentic, suggesting a light topic for the conversation that helps the participants relax.

Stock imagery
For any non-Roche employee-type imagery, please use a quality source for any ‘stock’ assets – do not use free image platforms, as copyright, product licensing and model releases might have not been signed. Unsplash.com & Pexels.com are just two examples of where _not_ to go for stock imagery.
Staging
Often within photoshoot logistics, staging is necessary, due to production timelines. So in order to achieve a natural composition, repetitive actions might be required. To ensure that the story of the image remains authentic, incorporate the below principles.

Real work tasks
The images should feel ‘active’ as if the subjects are involved in a real task. Their expressions should be natural, with a focus on the task being carried out. This helps our imagery feel natural and represents the reality of a Roche working environment.

Real moments
Our imagery captures a ‘moment’ in time, this means that people’s faces, gestures and actions should look authentic and engaged.

Correct uniform
Ensure that all staff are wearing the correct level of personal protective equipment and garments carry the correct branding (Order new or freshly cleaned and ironed items and ensure they are handed out prior to the shoot commencing).

Our imagery always takes into consideration the Roche SHE (Safety, Security, Health and Environmental protection) guidelines. This refers to the usage of, correct equipment, protection, process and even handling procedures of items. Please ensure you are familiar with or are supported by a consultant, prior to your production.

Rule of Thirds
Using the rule of thirds is an easy compositional tool that can apply to most images.

90-degree angles
Vertical and horizontal alignment should be at 90-degree angles when composing and cropping an image. If necessary, this can be adjusted during the retouching stage. The Roche Angle can also be used as a compositional tool.

Negative space
Providing a clear focus on the subject, with negative space is essential within our image composition. It also provides flexibility for future use in brand communications, print and digital, where typography or information may have to work on top of an image. This can be achieved using light or coloured areas of an image, or by using a shallow depth of field to create blurred areas.

Clean and tidy spaces
It is important that work environments appear clean and uncluttered and modern, scouting prior to your production helps avoid any surprises. Remove everything unnecessary and distracting to the eye. Simplify imagery, temporarily remove any notices, posters, labelling, remove any references to the ‘year’ where possible, so our images are less easily dated.

Camera positioning is not just for ‘people’ imagery
An important visual aspect of our brand photography style that helps connect with our audiences, is keeping the camera at (or as close to) eye level and level to the ground. Avoid framing from above or below perspectives, which causes a 'visual unease'. 

When camera positioning becomes awkward due to the environment or access to space is reduced, we can use the ‘macro & abstract’ style to crop in from a different viewing angle without causing visual unease.

The Roche Angle, a unique 49-degree that has been taken from the blueprint of the Roche logo, can be used as a compositional tool within Roche imagery. This creates a subtle yet elegant connection to the Roche hexagon.
Incorporating the Roche angle
As the subject of abstract and macro images have a high graphic appeal, incorporating the Roche angle works well and makes our photography more unique. The 49-degree angle can be incorporated from either edge of the Roche hexagon, both top left to bottom right, and bottom left to top right. This enables us to have a variety of angles in our abstract images. These are taken straight-on without perspective to the focal plane.

Cropping can enhance the composition and focus. When shooting brand images, it can often be helpful to allow additional space around the subject to ensure flexibility for the type of use in various media. This does not apply to the Hero portrait style.

Enhancing compositions
Cropping enables you to adjust the composition of an image for a stronger, more powerful photograph. It can also help to make an image appear calm and uncluttered when shooting in a complex or busy environment.

Landscape and portrait
The majority of Roche images are landscape but it’s important to capture enough space around the subject to allow for cropping that results in a portrait image. Take both formats when producing Hero portraits.

Horizontal and vertical lines
Cropping and rotating may also be necessary to ensure horizontal and vertical lines are true to the image frame.

Composition
Using a range of crops is helpful to create a dynamic and interesting set of images that feature people. Crops can be very close-up, focusing on a conversation or emotion, or they can use a wide crop, giving an overall impression of a scene and people working together.

Close-up
When the image uses a close-up crop it helps if one person is the focus of the image.

Wide crop
When an image uses a wide crop it is most successful if the entire group of people are in focus. Depth of field can still be incorporated within the image if shot through a doorway to achieve focus or ‘clear space’ can be created on the edge of an image.

It helps to create a strong focal point, it creates a contrast between the subject in focus and the environment, allowing the central narrative to be brought to light, and guides the way people look and read into our images.

Simplification
Using depth of field can also help make a busy space feel simplified by blurring out the complex elements.

Elegance
When used within abstract images it can also add elegance to an object that otherwise may seem quite ordinary.

Usage flexibility
It is a useful photographic technique to incorporate negative space to allow for the placement of type across applications, from print to digital.

Focus
Focusing on one part of a scene draws the viewer in, giving the impression they are part of it. This also applies to an image with more individuals creating a sense of calmness and simplicity.

Roche images should have a consistent approach to lighting and colour.

Natural light
Whenever possible, use natural daylight or techniques that simulate natural light. Through natural light, we add warmth to our imagery. When shooting indoors it helps to move closer to the source, next to windows or natural light sources to achieve the colour required. Alternatively, light can be edited in post-production if necessary.

Colour
The colour of the images should feel rich. We want to avoid cool/blue-looking imagery, which can often be associated with laboratory environments. Use colour photography, never black and white or duotone imagery. Colour balance must be consistent with a natural vibrancy and never over or under saturated. Be creative by using props in the fore or background, helping to add warmth to our imagery.

White space
The use of white or clear space is also important in our imagery, to give the feeling of simplicity within a composition.

- It is essential with each photoshoot to consider the narrative we want our images to tell
- Be aware of processes and information that are specific to each environment; these stories help to create a sense of narrative and purpose within each image
- Think about different and memorable ways to explain complex ideas and processes
- When photographing a laboratory, facility or building it’s good to document different stages of the process to build a story, step-by-step

Retouching is just as important as maintaining the authentic feel of the image once retouching is completed.

People and skin details
We remove only the most distracting elements in clothing, creases, folds, dirt, dust and hairs, when cleaning imagery of people, we aim to keep everything as natural as possible _and restrict the cleaning of people's features and skin to the minimum, _focusing on imperfections such as fly-away hairs, pimples (acne), razor rash, and specific blemishes requested by the individual. Please never use blurring to smooth skin as the natural textures are lost.
Angles
If using the 49-degree angle as a compositional tool, ensure it is accurate. Make sure horizontal lines are completely level and vertical lines are at a 90-degree angle to the image frame.

Roche branding
Where possible, update the Roche branding to the latest logo on applications such as uniforms or signage.

Clean environment
Elements in the background that don’t add to the narrative of the image and make it feel cluttered should be removed e.g. dirt, scuff marks, wear and tear on any equipment.

Timeless
Look out for any dates or items within images (e.g. cell phones, laptops) that may make them feel dated and, where possible, remove these elements.

Roche imagery is captured using the latest equipment to ensure the best possible level of quality. The minimum requirement is to use a full-frame sensor camera (mirrorless or DSLR) with a resolution of at least 24-megapixels, but preferably higher.

Lenses are just as important as the camera body and using glass that is of a high optical quality lends itself to adding a ‘look and feel’ to our imagery that non-prime lenses lack. A prime lens is for example required when capturing our ‘Hero’ portraits, where we use a 50mm fixed- prime lens with an aperture of ƒ2.2. Other content requires using a zoom lens with a focal range between 24 to 70mm. Do not use telephoto lenses above this as this produces too much compression between the subject and the background. We reserve the use of wider angle lenses for environmental or architectural photography only.

Always capture your data in both native RAW and Jpg file formats and provide both to your contact. Jpegs can be saved at a size of the longest edge 4000-pixels and with a reduced resolution of 200dpi for the review and selection process. Be prepared to provide the RAW data (not .dng files) to your contact when requested if the colour grading and/or post-production will be completed by another supplier.

When submitting your photography assets for inclusion in the brand platform, the minimum file requirements are:300dpi resolution, Adobe RGB 1998 ColourSpace with a minimum file size of 28-Megabytes, saved as LZW Compressed TIFS.

Retouching is required to be completed to the highest of standards. Always work on layers non-destructively, keeping all original masks/paths and alpha channels, keeping each revision as a separate file. When requested, you will also provide unrestricted access to the data as ‘open data’, fully layered photoshop, PSD or Layered TIF files (simplifying your layers is accepted, flattening completely is not).

Please keep a copy of your data as an archival backup (made available upon request) for as long as your contract with Roche is valid.
