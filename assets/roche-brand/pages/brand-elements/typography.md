The Roche typeface has a tier system.

The majority of Roche materials and templates utilise a limited set of font styles and weights to ensure accessibility and simplicity of design. 

‘Expert users’ who include designers and agencies can use the entire font family in exceptional circumstances where the usage provides added design value.

Below shows the extended Sans family of weights. Spanning a range of 4 weights, from Light to Bold, Roche Sans reinterprets how width and dimensions react within a conventional typeface family. 

At the lighter end of the family, the design is compressed, with flat edges allowing for clear distinction between forms. The Light and Regular styles are also accompanied by even more exaggerated space saving in the form of supporting Condensed styles. 

As the family progresses through the weight range to Medium & Bold, the type evokes friendlier, more human qualities, becoming more geometric and generous in its design. To give the Roche brand a modern and minimal appearance the Sans weights are key to all communication.
Typeface usage – Roche Sans
Roche Sans Light is to be used for display type, headlines, intros and body text. 

Roche Regular is to be used in subheadings and captioning. 

The Italic examples on this page should only be used for editorial styling.

This page shows the extended Serif family of weights. An expressive transitional design encompassing three weights, from a low contrast Light, to Regular and eventually glyphic Bold styles. Each weight is accompanied by calligraphic-inspired Italic styles that bring extra emphasis and expression to their respective weights. 

A versatile range of both Display and Body typefaces act as an accompaniment to the Roche Sans. Roche Serif is heroed within items such as quotes, information design or small copy.
Typeface usage – Serif
Roche Serif Light can be used for pull quotes, information design, and footnotes.

Roche Serif Regular is to be used by expert users only for setting type when more contrast is needed.

Roche Serif Bold is to be used by expert users only for setting type when more contrast is needed. 

The Italic examples on this page should only be used for editorial styling with the exception of Roche Serif Light which can be used to create large pull quotes in print by expert users.

The horizontal space in between the letters can include one of three different measurements to maximise legibility.

**Kerning:** adjusting the space between a pair of letters, numerals or punctuation must always be set to Metric.

**Tracking:** adjusting the spacing between all letter forms or glyphs within your text, is important to consider when text is being used within supergraphics and signage. Specific spacing between sets of characters can optimise text, increasing readability and aesthetics.

**Wordspacing: **adjusting the amount of space between each word, should also be considered to optimise the text.

**Leading:** is the vertical distance between the baselines of successive lines of type. This needs to be altered depending on the scale and weight of the type being set. The leading needs to be greater than the tracking and word spacing to optimise the legibility of the text.

It is important to ensure colours used for text are accessible throughout all print and digital applications when used for information design, headings, or UI design.

This section provides a reference guide for colour combinations that are accessible, and for those which are not. In some cases only large text is accessible and this is indicated with an asterisk system as shown below.

Passes AAA (strictest level, no symbol) for all text

** Passes AAA for large text only, but AA for normal text

*** Passes AA for large text only

To approach accessibility with the utmost seriousness, no combinations of colours are even provided that would rank lower than AA.

WCAG 2.0 level AA requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

WCAG Level AAA requires a contrast ratio of at least 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text.

“Large text” is defined as 14 point (typically 18.66px) in bold, or 18 point (typically 24px).
